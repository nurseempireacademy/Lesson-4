01The Real Math

The Math Nobody Showed You

Here is the moment most nurses have when they first look at 1099 income: they see $85 an hour, compare it to their current $45 an hour, and think they are about to make an extra $40 an hour.

That math is wrong. Not because $85 is not real — it is. But because that $85 now has to cover things your employer was quietly paying for on your behalf.

This chapter shows you the real number. How much you actually keep. What you owe and when. And how to set yourself up so the IRS does not surprise you in April with a bill that erases months of progress.

02Self-Employment Tax

Self-Employment Tax — What It Is and Why It Hits Hard

When you work as a W-2 employee, your employer splits the Social Security and Medicare taxes with you. You each pay 7.65%. The employer's half never shows up on your pay stub — it just gets paid behind the scenes.

When you are a 1099 contractor, there is no employer to split with. You pay the full 15.3%. That is 12.4% for Social Security (on income up to $184,500 in 2026) plus 2.9% for Medicare on all income.

The good news: you can deduct half of your self-employment tax on your federal income tax return — which reduces your taxable income. But that deduction comes later. The money still leaves your account first.

04Quarterly Taxes

Quarterly Taxes — The Four Dates That Run Your Year

As a 1099 contractor, the IRS expects you to pay taxes four times a year — not once in April. Missing a quarterly payment triggers an underpayment penalty, even if you pay in full at tax time. Put these dates in your phone right now.

Figure 4 — 2026 Quarterly Tax Payment Calendar
Q1 Payment
April 15, 2026
Covers income earned Jan 1 – March 31
Same day as your annual return. Two due dates in one — plan ahead.
Q2 Payment
June 15, 2026
Covers income earned April 1 – May 31
Only two months of income — shorter window. Set money aside weekly.
Q3 Payment
Sept 15, 2026
Covers income earned June 1 – Aug 31
Summer quarter. Three months. Consistent income tracking is essential.
Q4 Payment
Jan 15, 2027
Covers income earned Sept 1 – Dec 31
Holiday season = higher spending. Do not touch your tax reserve in Q4.
Pay via: IRS Direct Pay at IRS.gov (free, instant) or EFTPS.gov (Electronic Federal Tax Payment System). Set up EFTPS before your first quarterly payment — it takes a few days to activate. Texas has no state income tax, so no state quarterly payments are required.
05The Three Accounts

The Three Accounts You Open Before Your First Check Clears

This is the system that keeps nurses from getting blindsided in April. Three separate accounts. One simple rule for each. Set it up once and it runs on autopilot.

Figure 5 — Your Three-Account System
1
Business Operating Account
Your main business checking
All client payments land here first
Every invoice you send gets paid into this account. From here, you immediately transfer your tax reserve and retirement contribution. What remains is your operating income to pay yourself and cover business expenses.
2
Tax Reserve Account
Business savings — do not touch
Transfer 25–30% of every payment immediately
Set up an automatic transfer the moment a payment clears. This money does not exist for spending. It exists for your quarterly IRS payments. When April comes, you are prepared instead of panicked. Relay lets you label sub-accounts — name this one "IRS — Do Not Touch."
3
Retirement Account
Solo 401(k) or SEP-IRA
Contribute from every payment — even small amounts
No employer is contributing to your retirement anymore. That is now your responsibility — and your opportunity. Contributions to a Solo 401(k) or SEP-IRA reduce your taxable income dollar for dollar. Every dollar saves you money in taxes this year and builds wealth for the future.
06Retirement Accounts

Building Wealth — Retirement Accounts for Self-Employed Nurses

One of the most overlooked advantages of self-employment: as a 1099 contractor, you can contribute dramatically more to retirement accounts than you could as a W-2 employee. A traditional 401(k) through an employer caps your contribution at $23,500 in 2026. As a self-employed business owner, your total ceiling is $70,000.

These contributions are tax-deductible — they reduce your taxable income in the year you make them. That means saving for retirement and lowering your tax bill simultaneously.

07Health Insurance

Health Insurance — You Are On Your Own Now (Here's What to Do)

This is the one that trips people up most. As a W-2 employee your employer was likely covering a significant portion of your health insurance premium. As a 1099 contractor that cost is entirely yours — but it is also 100% tax-deductible as a self-employed person, which reduces the real after-tax cost meaningfully.

Figure 7 — Health Insurance Options for Independent Nurses
ACA Marketplace (Healthcare.gov)
Gold or Silver plans available. Income-based subsidies may apply. Open Enrollment Nov 1 – Jan 15. Special Enrollment if you lose job-based coverage — you have 60 days to enroll.
$300–$600/mo avg before subsidies
Spouse or Domestic Partner Coverage
If your spouse or partner has employer-sponsored insurance, joining their plan is often the most cost-effective option. Compare the premium difference before assuming the marketplace is cheaper.
Varies — often lowest out-of-pocket
Professional Association Plans
AONL, ANA, and some state nursing associations offer group health plans for members. Rates are often competitive with ACA marketplace plans and sometimes better. Worth checking your memberships.
$200–$500/mo depending on plan
COBRA (Bridge Coverage)
Continue your current employer coverage for up to 18 months after leaving. You pay the full premium including the employer's share. Expensive — but provides continuity without a gap while you shop.
$600–$1,200/mo — temporary only
The Tax Deduction Most Nurses Miss Self-employed individuals can deduct 100% of health insurance premiums paid for themselves and their families from their federal income tax. This is an above-the-line deduction — meaning you do not have to itemize to claim it. On a $600/month premium, that is a $7,200 annual deduction. Source: IRS Publication 535.
08Deductions

Business Deductions — The Expenses That Lower Your Tax Bill

One of the most significant advantages of being a business owner: legitimate business expenses reduce your taxable income. You are not paying taxes on gross income — you are paying taxes on profit (income minus deductions). Here are the deductions most relevant to nurse entrepreneurs.

Figure 8 — Common Business Deductions for Nurse Entrepreneurs
01
Business Mileage
IRS standard rate for 2026. Track every mile driven for business. Apps: MileIQ or Everlance.
02
Home Office
Space used regularly and exclusively for your business. Simplified method: $5/sq ft up to 300 sq ft.
03
Continuing Education
CE courses, certifications, license renewal fees, professional books, and online courses related to your work.
04
Health Insurance Premiums
100% deductible for self-employed. Covers you, spouse, and dependents. Cannot exceed your net business income.
05
Professional Insurance
Malpractice, general liability, cyber liability — all fully deductible as ordinary business expenses.
06
Phone & Internet
The business-use percentage of your phone bill and home internet. Track and document the business-use percentage.
07
Software & AI Tools
Claude Pro, ChatGPT Plus, QuickBooks, scheduling tools — any software used for your business is deductible.
08
LLC & Legal Fees
LLC filing fees, registered agent fees, attorney fees for contracts, and accounting fees are all deductible.
09
Travel & Conferences
Flights, hotels, meals (50%), and registration fees for business-related conferences and professional events.
Document Everything The IRS does not accept your memory. Keep digital receipts for every business expense. Apps like Expensify or a dedicated Google Drive folder work well. Business mileage requires a log with date, destination, business purpose, and miles. Start tracking from your first day of business — not after.

Chapter 4 — The Key Points
1
Self-employment tax is 15.3% — 12.4% Social Security + 2.9% Medicare. You pay both halves. You can deduct half from your federal income tax. Source: IRS Publication 334 (2026).
2
Four quarterly payments per year. April 15 · June 15 · September 15 · January 15. Missing one triggers penalties. Pay via IRS Direct Pay at IRS.gov — it's free.
3
Set aside 25–30% of every payment immediately. Before you spend anything. Into a separate account labeled "Tax Reserve." This one habit prevents every April surprise.
4
Open three accounts: operating checking, tax reserve savings, and a retirement account (Solo 401(k) or SEP-IRA). Set up all three before your first client payment clears.
5

7
Hire a CPA. Not a tax-prep service — a CPA who works with self-employed healthcare professionals. The right CPA pays for themselves many times over in deductions you would have missed.
